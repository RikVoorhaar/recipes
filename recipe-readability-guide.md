# Designing recipes for the returning glance

**Summary.** Design the recipe for a cook who has just looked back from the pan. They should be able to find their place, see exactly what to add, and tell when to move on without reconstructing the last paragraph. Keep a complete ingredient list for gathering and measuring; repeat each step's own quantities at the point of use. Add a generated “Used in” index running in the opposite direction. Use numbers and descriptive headings as the main landmarks; let restrained color and weight reinforce them. The best direct evidence concerns integrated ingredient information, not a particular palette: in one cooking experiment mean ingredient lookups per session fell from **4.96 to 1.13**, without a significant difference in total completion time ([Buykx, chapter 6, especially Table 38](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/5158/1/Lucy%20Buykx%20accepted%20thesis.pdf)).

## Principles to build around

| Priority | Principle | What a cook should see or be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| Must | Answer both ingredient questions. | “Where does the butter go?” → **Steps 1, 3** in the list. “How much goes in now?” → **10 g butter** in step 3, without following a link. |
| Must | Give each step an address and a name. | **3 · Finish the sauce**, not “Part three” or a colored dot. Keep the same number and wording on the web and on paper for a given revision. |
| Must | Put decisions before irreversible actions. | “Reserve 100 ml cooking water, then drain,” not a note about the water after the pasta is in the colander. State prerequisites before the cook commits. |
| Must | Write to an observable end state. | Put the action, the relevant heat or duration, and what “done” means together. Distinguish “oven at 180 °C” from “centre reaches [target]”. Never invent precision. |
| Must | Preserve the whole route. | A cook can see what comes next, what can overlap, and where two components meet. No mandatory one-step-at-a-time wizard. |
| Must | Make the unstyled recipe work. | Strip color, bold, and interactivity: the amounts, order, component names, references and stopping conditions still make sense. |
| Should | Give the eye a few dependable landmarks. | Use a consistent number gutter, short action headings and generous space between steps. Reserve emphasis inside prose for information worth finding on a second glance. |
| Should | Design paper at its actual reading size. | Check a physical card on a kitchen counter. Passing a one-page fit test does not mean a cook can read it from the bowl. |
| Test | Add redundancy selectively. | Try matching phase accents and step links; retain them only if people find the right place more quickly without increasing wrong answers or crowding the card. |

These are design decisions, not nine experimentally proven rules. The sections below separate cooking evidence, accessibility requirements, and proposals to test. For the existing delivery contract, see the repository's [v1 specification](https://github.com/RikVoorhaar/recipes/blob/main/recipe-app-v1-spec.md); this guide does not amend it.

## Start with the ingredient-to-step relationship

A total, an allocation, and a reference are different facts. The list answers “what should I get out?”; the method answers “what do I use now?”; the reverse index answers “where is this used?” Giving all three jobs to a single cross-reference still makes the cook leave the step. Keeping related information together is supported directly by [Buykx's cooking studies](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/5158/); the total-plus-local-allocations convention is also recommended in [Virginia Tech's recipe-writing guide](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f7e9a819-d2e2-4f37-9a90-20c8b7c77596/content).

### Worked example: lemon-butter crumbs

**Gather:** Makes one small bowl. You need a frying pan and a plate. This deliberately short example demonstrates the print fallback as well as the web wording.

| Ingredient | Amount to gather | Used in |
|---|---:|---|
| Butter, divided | 30 g | Steps 1, 3 |
| Coarse breadcrumbs | 60 g | Step 1 |
| Finely grated lemon zest | 1 tsp | Step 3 |

1. **Toast the crumbs.** Melt **20 g butter** in the pan over medium heat. Add **60 g breadcrumbs** and stir until golden and crisp, about 3–5 min.
2. **Empty the pan.** Transfer the toasted crumbs to the plate. Take the pan off the heat.
3. **Finish with lemon.** Melt the remaining **10 g butter** in the warm pan. Stir in **1 tsp lemon zest**, then toss with the toasted crumbs.

Read only the list: you can measure the total butter and see that it will be divided. Read only step 3: you know exactly how much butter to use. The toasted crumbs in steps 2 and 3 are the output of step 1, **not more raw breadcrumbs**. If useful, render that as “toasted crumbs from step 1”; do not add steps 2 and 3 to the raw breadcrumbs' addition index. The action headings and allocations remain usable when printed without color. In a richer renderer, use semibold for the ingredient phrases rather than giving them the same visual weight as the headings.

### Editorial and rendering rules

- **Order and grouping:** Within each named component, list ingredients by first use, not alphabetically. Use the same component names in the method. Do not force components onto a three-ingredient recipe. First-use ordering and explicit part names follow [Virginia Tech's editorial guidance](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f7e9a819-d2e2-4f37-9a90-20c8b7c77596/content).
- **Split quantities:** Show the total once and each local amount at the action. For a complicated split, the list can say “30 g total: 20 g for crumbs; 10 g to finish.” For a simple split, “divided · Steps 1, 3” is enough. Do not write just “half” or “the rest” when the exact allocation is available.
- **Many destinations:** Keep references in reading order and label their meaning, e.g. “Add in steps 1, 4, 7.” Do not render “1–7” if intermediate steps are not uses. For a recipe with many additions, group by named component or show local portions under one total rather than making a long rainbow of badges.
- **Ambiguous amounts:** Keep “plus extra for dusting,” “to taste,” optional garnishes, fixed quantities and alternatives explicit at their destination. Do not silently add an unknown amount into a numerical total or count “butter or oil” as two required ingredients. If compatible units cannot be added by the supported formatter, show separate allocations rather than guessing a conversion.
- **Ingredients versus operations:** Distinguish adding, reserving, using a reserved portion, and handling a prepared mixture. For example, stock taken from an earlier pot is not a second purchase; “reserve before draining” must survive even if the amount is approximate. Give a prepared component a stable human name, such as “cooled custard,” instead of using “it” across a step boundary.
- **No false structure:** For a multi-step recipe, generate the index even when an ingredient has only one destination. For an ingredient-list-only recipe, omit the “Used in” column entirely, and for a single step omit redundant “Step 1” on every line. Never invent a step to satisfy a layout.

## Write instructions the cook can re-enter

Use a step for a meaningful subgoal, not a sentence quota. As a working rule, start a new step when the cook changes vessel, can walk away, switches component, or must verify a state before proceeding. Keep tightly coupled actions together when splitting them would create more page hunting than clarity. In Buykx's experiment, shortening steps alone did not significantly improve clarity or ease-of-use ratings over the conventional presentation; cooks also read ahead rather than simply executing text top to bottom ([Buykx, chapters 6–7](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/5158/1/Lucy%20Buykx%20accepted%20thesis.pdf)).

| Instead of | Write or display | Why |
|---|---|---|
| “Preparation” repeated three times | “Toast the nuts”, “Whisk the filling”, “Fill the shell” | The first content words distinguish the targets. A numbered heading is an address; the verb and object tell the cook whether it is the right address. |
| “Bake until done, about 25 minutes” | “Bake until the edges are set and the centre still wobbles slightly; start checking at 25 min” if appropriate for the tested recipe | Separates a check time from the actual stopping condition. Do not manufacture a cue the author has not verified. |
| “Meanwhile, make the sauce” at the end of baking | “While the squash roasts (step 2), make the sauce.” Put this at the beginning of the sauce step, and signpost it from step 2. | Tells the cook which wait creates the opportunity. Distinguish optional parallel work from a required dependency. |
| “Add chopped nuts” when the measure is ambiguous | “Weigh 60 g walnuts, then chop” or “Measure ½ cup chopped walnuts,” whichever was tested | Measurement before and after preparation is not interchangeable. Keep the relevant state with the amount. |
| A late note: “Best if chilled overnight” | At the top: “Start the day before: chill overnight.” At the step: “Cover and chill overnight before slicing.” | A scheduling constraint belongs at the planning decision and at execution, not only in endnotes. |
| “Heat to 180°” | “Heat the oven to 180 °C (fan)” if that is the tested setting | Name what is measured, where relevant how, and in which unit. A pan-surface reading and a core reading are not substitutes. |

**Plan without doing arithmetic.** Give yield and truly consequential prerequisites before the ingredient list: pan size if it affects depth, a required thermometer, chilling or thawing, or something to reserve. Offer an authored active/elapsed time distinction if available. Do not sum all timers when tasks overlap, and do not promise an elapsed time if proofing or chilling is genuinely variable.

**Support experienced cooks without cryptic shorthand.** Keep common techniques concise; spend words on unusual order, easily missed quantities, and irreversible decisions. Add an alternative to a purely visual cue when the author knows one: for example, “a skewer slides through the potato without resistance” describes a check without relying on color. Blind cooks and adaptive cooking instructors specifically identified precise descriptions, time ranges, alternatives to visual cues, and logical chunks as useful; this was interview and recipe-editing evidence, not a timed trial ([Li et al., ASSETS 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19065)).

## A visual language for scanning

Build a recipe that works in black ink first. Add a visual signal only when you can name the reader's question it answers. Visual-search research describes guidance from both distinctive features and expectations about where to look; web eye-tracking also shows readers using informative, visually distinct headings to select relevant passages ([Wolfe, Guided Search 6.0](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965574/); [Nielsen Norman Group, layer-cake scanning](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/)). Neither establishes an optimal number of colors or a best font for a recipe card.

| Role | Proposed treatment on both surfaces | Misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Step address | Fixed leading gutter; clear numeral plus short bold verb–object heading. Align continuation lines with the text, not the number. | Number alone as a heading; a different position in every step. |
| Ingredient occurrence | In prose, semibold the amount, unit and ingredient **as one phrase** on introduction or addition. In the aligned ingredient list, regular weight is often enough. | Bolding every repeat, bolding numerals without units, or styling every noun like a link. |
| Time and temperature | Keep value, unit and referent together: “oven 180 °C”, “rest 10 min”. In a busy step, emphasize the decisive value or use a short labeled line; don't duplicate all numbers in a second badge row. | One isolated “180°” chip or a timer chip so prominent it eclipses “until set”. |
| Phase identity | For several components, use a narrow accent beside the phase or number and repeat that accent on related references. Always print the phase name and step number. | A unique hue for every step of a 15-step recipe, a legend to memorize, or colored paragraphs. |
| Current position | On web, use a labeled “Current” marker plus a strong border or shape independent of phase color. On paper, keep a clean number gutter suitable for a pencil tick or movable marker. | Treating “currently reading”, “completed” and “timer running” as the same state. |
| Exception | Start a short line with an explicit instruction such as **Reserve before draining:**. Reserve strong contrast and spacing for consequential exceptions. | Relegating the exception to italics, a faint note, red text alone, or a footnote. |
| Optional note | Use regular, readable text after the action, prefixed “Optional:” or “Why:”. Leave narrative and substitutions outside the execution path unless needed now. | Long italic passages, a glossary of font weights, or essential information visible only on hover. |

**Color-code the location, not the meaning of the sentence.** For example, give “Dough · Steps 1–3” one accent and “Filling · Steps 4–5” another. The ingredient row can say “Step 4” using the filling accent; the matching heading still reads “4 · Whisk the filling” in grayscale. For a four-step, single-phase recipe where steps look similar, test small distinct number-marker accents against one neutral accent. Do not make cooks learn “purple means whisk”; they should be able to match or ignore color. Treat both palette choices as hypotheses.

**Use whitespace before adding more ink.** Put less space between a heading and its own instructions than between adjacent steps. Use left alignment, a common starting edge, and enough room that “1 tsp” cannot be mistaken for the quantity on the next row. Let long ingredient names wrap within their row. Try a readable serif if it suits the family book; judge actual numerals, fractions, degree signs, x-height and weight differences at final size. UKAAF recommends left alignment, discourages italics, and does not identify a universal serif-versus-sans winner ([UKAAF clear-print guidance](https://www.ukaaf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/G003-UKAAF-Creating-clear-print-and-large-print-documents-v4.pdf)).

## Same information, different surfaces

| Design question | Web recipe | Printed recipe |
|---|---|---|
| Where do I start? | Title, yield, consequential timing, then visible jumps to “Ingredients” and “Method”. Keep the image for dish recognition, but do not force a scroll through narrative before cooking. | Title, yield, recognizable dish image when provided, and a distinct start to the method. The picture serves family selection; it need not dominate every inch of the execution surface. |
| Can I see ingredients and actions? | At wide widths, use aligned list and method columns if both remain comfortable to read. At narrow widths, use normal single-column flow with amounts inline; don't shrink desktop columns onto a phone. | Use columns only if neither column chops up ingredient names or method phrases. On a narrow A5 proof, first try a full-width method beneath a compact ingredient block. |
| How do I follow a reference? | Use ordinary step anchors with accessible names such as “Step 3: Finish with lemon”. After a jump, retain a clear target and support return to the originating row and scroll position. No tooltip-only allocations. | References are literal text, e.g. “Steps 1, 3”. They remain useful even when links, color, backgrounds and icons disappear. |
| How do I return after an interruption? | Optional “Mark current step”, independent of scrolling and completion. Preserve it when inspecting ingredients; provide an obvious return control. Don't auto-advance on a timer; multiple components may be live. | Fixed step positions and short headings do the work. Leave space for a tick. Never use a separate key on the reverse just to decode the front. |
| What if the cook changes yield? | Update list totals, local allocations and displayed yield together; keep the cook's place. Fixed amounts remain fixed. Download exactly the yield being viewed. | Print the actual yield prominently. Paper cannot recalculate, so local quantities must already agree with the list. |
| What do images contribute? | Put a process image beside the decision it resolves, with useful text describing the state. Keep decorative imagery out of the reading path in cooking mode. | Keep the family-recognition image. A second image must earn its space: show a difficult fold or target consistency, not another view of the finished dish. Never put the only doneness cue in a picture. |

### Web accessibility acceptance checks

- **Contrast and color:** Target WCAG 2.2 AA. Do not use color alone to convey information (1.4.1). Normal text needs at least 4.5:1 contrast; large-scale text at least 3:1 (1.4.3). Necessary graphical objects and identifying information for relevant UI controls/states generally need 3:1 against adjacent colors (1.4.11); not every decorative border does. Test every chip, active state, and theme against its actual background ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)).
- **Zoom and interaction:** Test text resizing to 200% without lost content or functionality (1.4.4) and vertical content at 320 CSS px wide without two-dimensional scrolling (1.4.10, with the criterion's exceptions). Test keyboard operation, visible focus, and focus not entirely obscured by sticky content. For discrete controls, 24 × 24 CSS px is the AA target-size baseline subject to spacing and other exceptions; prefer larger, separated controls for cooking, not a cluster of minimum-size buttons ([WCAG 2.2, including 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)).
- **Structure:** Use real headings, ordered steps, ingredient lists or properly headed tables, and meaningful link names. Check the actual screen-reader order on the narrow layout, including step references, fractions, quantities and timer labels. Don't announce every decorative accent or force someone through a series of duplicate “Step 3” links to reach the method ([WCAG 2.2, especially 1.3.1, 1.3.2 and 2.4.4](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/); [Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19065)).
- **Kitchen trial:** Prototype a body size near 1.125 rem and comfortable line spacing as adjustable starting points, not standards. Put the phone in a stand, use it one-handed, enlarge the text, and follow a split-ingredient step without tapping. A sticky ingredient panel is expendable if it hides the next action.

### Print: distinguish fitting from being legible

The [v1 spec, sections 10–11 and acceptance tests](https://github.com/RikVoorhaar/recipes/blob/main/recipe-app-v1-spec.md) requires one A4 page, starts body text at 11 pt, allows reduction to 8 pt, and rejects overflow rather than adding a page; A5 and duplex are deferred. By contrast, [UKAAF's clear-print guidance](https://www.ukaaf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/G003-UKAAF-Creating-clear-print-and-large-print-documents-v4.pdf) recommends at least 12 pt, ideally 14 pt, for clear print. Those are **different thresholds for different goals**. Do not describe an 8 pt sheet as clear print, and do not silently change v1's page contract.

- **Current A4 output:** Keep the required fit result, but expose the selected type size to the author and flag “fits, but below clear-print target” separately from an overflow error. Cut redundant prose and furniture, simplify references, and adjust spacing or image area before squeezing critical instructions. Never remove a divided amount, a condition or a temperature to save a line.
- **Future A5 output:** Set a new master at actual A5 size rather than printing the A4 PDF at roughly 71% linear scale: 11 pt would become about 7.8 pt. Start a proof at 12–14 pt body, try one column, and make fit failure an editorial decision. If a recipe cannot fit a single legible face, prefer a concise rewrite, a larger sheet, or an explicitly approved multi-face design; do not silently paginate v1.
- **Physical proof:** Print at 100%, in color and monochrome, on an ordinary printer. Inspect at the distance and angle at which the cook actually works, under ordinary kitchen lighting, not only in a PDF viewer. Check fractional glyphs, faint accents, long names, the gutter, the smallest “Used in” labels and the image after conversion. Reject a design that requires colored ink to find step 3.

## Generate the layout from one recipe, not two

The [v1 spec, sections 3–6 and 10–11](https://github.com/RikVoorhaar/recipes/blob/main/recipe-app-v1-spec.md) already puts authored quantities in the Cooklang body, derives ingredient occurrences, and shares scaling and formatting between web and PDF. The following is a proposed renderer contract, not new Cooklang syntax. A markup experiment by Peter Flynn offers a useful precedent for linking ingredient mentions by identity and checking consistency; it was an engineering demonstration, not a cook usability trial ([Flynn, “Cooking up something new”](https://www.balisage.net//Proceedings//vol25/print/Flynn01/BalisageVol25-Flynn01.html)).

| Derived information | Rule |
|---|---|
| Step address | Enumerate executable steps in the displayed revision, globally across components. Generate links, headings and print references from that same ordering. Use revision-aware keys for bookmarks; after an edit, do not silently restore “current” to an unrelated step. |
| Ingredient occurrence | Associate each authored occurrence with its step, displayed wording, canonical identity, component, quantity/unit and applicable scaling behavior. A search alias is not permission to combine differently prepared ingredients or substitute one for another. |
| Kind of use | Distinguish “add this amount” from “refer to an earlier amount or component.” Derive from supported parser semantics when available; where text is ambiguous, return an author warning or request an explicit reference, not a guessed quantity. |
| Forward view | Render each addition inside its step from its occurrence. Keep a local inline quantity even when the total is already displayed elsewhere. Mark non-quantified uses honestly. |
| Reverse view | Group compatible additions for gathering; collect distinct step addresses in display order. Link optional uses and named components with clear labels. A reference to an already-mixed component is not a second addition of all its constituents. |
| One computation | Scale once per revision and yield, then use the same structured result for HTML, Typst and validation. Detect materially misleading rounding; do not “fix” a discrepancy by silently changing the last portion. |

**Validation fixtures.** Start with the butter example above. At double yield the result must be 60 g total, 40 g in step 1, 20 g in step 3, with no raw breadcrumbs counted in steps 2–3. Insert a new first step and verify that both renderers now point butter to steps 2 and 4. Add fixtures for an unquantified garnish, an optional alternative, fixed plus scaled quantities, incompatible units, two preparations of one ingredient, a reserved portion, and a recipe with no instructions. Validate the rendered text, not just the presence of an internal ID.

**Authoring lint.** Block broken references and impossible render states. Warn about suspicious unused or unidentified mentions, “remaining” without an identifiable earlier split, or a cook-critical instruction buried in a note. Present the step and reason to the MCP author; do not silently rewrite the recipe. Incomplete ingredient descriptions are a documented problem in recipe corpora, and identification is not equivalent to proving a recipe correct ([“Defining and Detecting Incomplete Ingredient Descriptions”](https://aclanthology.org/2024.paclic-1.43.pdf)).

## What the research establishes, and where it stops

This is a targeted design review, not a systematic review. Give greatest weight to observations of people cooking for execution questions, to accessibility standards for web requirements, and to physical proofs for print decisions. A preference, a quicker answer on a monitor, a learning score and a better finished dish are different outcomes.

| Evidence | Finding that matters here | Boundary on the inference |
|---|---|---|
| Direct: [Lucy Buykx, University of York, 2013, chapters 6–7](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/5158/1/Lucy%20Buykx%20accepted%20thesis.pdf) | In the first cooking experiment (24 cooks), mean ingredient lookups were 4.96 with the conventional presentation, 3.91 with segmented steps, and 1.13 with integrated information. The integrated version was rated clearer and easier than control. In a separate cooking experiment, participants valued an overview for understanding subgoals and planning parallel work. | Young adult samples and a limited set of recipes. No significant overall completion-time or error benefit in the first experiment. Integration was studied; our particular reverse-index design was not. |
| Direct, simulated retrieval: [M. Monroe, EuroVis 2016](https://diglib.eg.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/c1aa6557-e3e8-41ff-96cb-e56be4b19697/content) | Twenty technically inclined participants answered six cooking questions using conventional and workflow layouts. With the workflow they produced nearly twice as many correct answers in about half the time. | Two pasta recipes, a large monitor, a tutorial, and no actual cooking. The layout bundled several changes. This supports testing retrieval tasks, not making a flowchart the default or promising a 2× gain. |
| Direct, qualitative: [Li, Wang, Carrington & Kane, ASSETS 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.19065) | Interviews with 20 cooks with vision impairments and four instructors, plus feedback on recipes, identified navigation, precise language, nonvisual cues, and the cost of touching a device with occupied hands. | These findings reveal needs, not which color or control achieved the fastest use. Include nonvisual users in testing rather than treating a contrast audit as a substitute. |
| Direct, exploratory: [van der Sluis & Mellema, multimodal baking instructions](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/26349795241305281) | A corpus of 15 blogs, a 12-person reading study and four baking teams let the authors distinguish judgments about illustrated instructions from their use in baking. Pictures can show a result against which readers check their work. | Small samples and cookie recipes; preference for illustration does not establish better baking performance. Keep recognition photos and diagnostic process photos as separate design decisions. |
| Transfer: [Schroeder & Cenkci, spatial contiguity meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9) and [Schneider et al., signaling meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X17300581) | Across multimedia learning studies, spatial integration of related information and cues to relevant information generally benefited learning. These are plausible mechanisms for keeping measurements with actions and emphasizing a small number of targets. | These are learning outcomes, often involving words and diagrams. They are not estimates of seconds saved in a kitchen, nor evidence that more emphasis is always better. |
| Transfer: [Wolfe, Guided Search 6.0](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965574/), [NN/g, heading-based scanning](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/layer-cake-pattern-scanning/), and [Trafton et al., interruption cues](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/154193120504900354) | Visual search can be guided by distinctive features and learned structure; headings support selective web reading; in a laboratory interruption experiment, a conspicuous place cue sped resumption while a subtle one did not. | These results motivate a clear number gutter, descriptive headings and an explicit current-step marker. They do not validate specific recipe colors, prove a bookmark must be red, or establish that every cook wants a tracker. |
| Standards and professional practice: [W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/), [UKAAF clear print](https://www.ukaaf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/G003-UKAAF-Creating-clear-print-and-large-print-documents-v4.pdf), [Virginia Tech recipe guide](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f7e9a819-d2e2-4f37-9a90-20c8b7c77596/content) | Use WCAG for testable web criteria, UKAAF for print legibility targets, and editorial guidance for ingredient order, component labels and divided quantities. | A standard sets a floor or a convention; it does not establish the best typeface, palette, or page density for this particular kitchen. |

**Open questions.** The reviewed work does not settle whether a dedicated “Used in” column beats a quiet reference at the end of each row, whether phase colors beat per-step colors for short recipes, or whether semibold ingredient phrases beat plain text on small cards. Those are inexpensive, consequential choices to test. The evidence for putting the amount at the action is stronger than the evidence for any of these treatments.

## Validate with the tasks the page is meant to support

First compare a conventional list-plus-method baseline with the same recipe using inline amounts. Then hold the inline method constant and compare no index versus a neutral numbered index. Finally compare that index with matching phase or step accents. Keep wording, size and spacing identical wherever the comparison allows; otherwise a win for larger text can be mistaken for a win for color.

| Ask the cook | Record | Common false success |
|---|---|---|
| “Which steps use butter? How much do you need in total?” | Time to first correct answer, all destinations found, wrong inclusions | Naming only its first use. |
| “You are at step 3. How much goes in now?” | Correct local amount and unit; returns to list, scrolls or page turns | Quoting the total, or answering faster but incorrectly. |
| After a short unrelated task: “Where were you, and what comes next?” | Time from looking back to the correct resumed action, wrong-step starts | Finding the most vivid step rather than the actual one. |
| “What can you do during the wait? What has to happen before draining?” | Valid parallel action and correct dependency, not just recalled wording | Spotting the timer but missing the irreversible prerequisite. |
| “How will you know it's ready?” | Correct stopping cue and measurement location where applicable | Giving only a clock time when the text requires a state check. |

Use a simple recipe and a multi-component one with a split ingredient; include one list-only case to catch needless interface. Rotate equivalent recipes and presentation order so participants do not answer from memory. Test on a real phone and with actual-size A4/A5 proofs, including monochrome; include experienced and less experienced cooks, people using large text, and nonvisual web navigation. Do a short lookup session first, then cook a dish to catch failures that do not appear at a desk.

**Decision rule.** Prioritize correct amounts, order and stopping conditions over speed. Among equally accurate layouts, prefer the one with shorter median time to a correct answer, fewer cross-lookups and fewer interactions; inspect slow or failed trials individually. Ask about effort and preference after the tasks, not instead of observing them. Ship inline amounts and meaningful landmarks first; keep extra badges, color and progress controls only when they pay for the space or interaction they consume.
